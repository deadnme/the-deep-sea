The Deep Sea

Nearly 11 kilometres of water separate the surface from the deepest point on Earth. Scroll to sink through it, zone by zone, and meet what lives down there.

Scroll to descend
0 – 200 m

Sunlight Zone

Epipelagic

Enough sunlight reaches this thin top layer for photosynthesis, so almost all of the ocean's plant life grows here. Food is plentiful, and so are the animals that eat it.

200 – 1,000 m

Twilight Zone

Mesopelagic

Light fades to a dim blue, too weak for photosynthesis. Many animals here make their own light to hunt, hide or find mates.

Each night, billions of them swim up toward the surface to feed and sink back at dawn: the largest migration on Earth.

1,000 – 4,000 m

Midnight Zone

Bathypelagic

No sunlight reaches this far. The only light is bioluminescence. The water sits near 4 °C, and the pressure is more than 100 times that at the surface.

4,000 – 6,000 m

Abyssal Zone

Abyssopelagic

The abyssal plains cover most of the ocean floor. The water is close to freezing, and most food arrives as "marine snow": flakes of dead matter drifting down from above.

6,000 – 11,000 m

Hadal Zone

Hadopelagic

Named after Hades, the Greek underworld. This zone exists only inside deep ocean trenches, most of them around the rim of the Pacific.

Below ~8,200 m

Beyond the Fish Limit

Too deep for any fish

Fish cannot survive much below about 8,200 m. Their bodies cannot hold enough of the molecule that protects their proteins from the pressure.

The deepest fish ever filmed, a snailfish, was swimming at 8,336 m in 2022. Below that, amphipods and sea cucumbers take over.

~10,935 m · Mariana Trench

Challenger Deep

The deepest known point on Earth

The pressure here is more than 1,000 times that at the surface. If Mount Everest stood on this floor, its peak would still be about 2 km underwater.

Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh first reached the bottom in 1960, in the bathyscaphe Trieste. Published depths range from about 10,920 to 10,994 m.
