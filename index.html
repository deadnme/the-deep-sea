The last of the light.

Twilight zone, 200 to 1,000 m, mesopelagic

Sunlight fades to a dim blue, too weak for plants to grow. Many animals here make their own light, and each night billions of them swim up to feed.

Darkness, with a spark.

Midnight zone, 1,000 to 4,000 m, bathypelagic

No sunlight reaches this deep. The only light comes from animals, like the glowing lure an anglerfish dangles to draw prey toward its jaws.

Cold, dark and crushing.

Abyssal zone, 4,000 to 6,000 m, abyssopelagic

The water is near freezing and the pressure is hundreds of times that at the surface. A dumbo octopus glides over the seafloor on ear-like fins.

Into the trenches.

Hadal zone, 6,000 to 11,000 m, hadopelagic

Named after Hades, this zone exists only inside deep ocean trenches. Pale snailfish live in its upper reaches, feeding on small crustaceans.

Beyond the fish limit.

Hadal zone, below about 8,200 m

No fish can survive much deeper than this. The deepest fish ever filmed, a snailfish, swam at 8,336 m. Below it, amphipods take over.

The deepest place we know.

Challenger Deep, about 10,935 m, Mariana Trench

The pressure here is more than 1,000 times that at the surface. Amphipods, sea cucumbers and giant single-celled xenophyophores still live on the sediment.

About this site

A little context.

This is an artistic, educational journey, not a reconstruction of a real dive. Creature shapes and sizes are stylized. Each zone gets about the same amount of scrolling, and the descent slows near featured creatures, so depth does not change at a constant rate.

Pressure is estimated at one atmosphere per 10 metres plus surface pressure. The endpoint uses NOAA's approximate depth; measurements of Challenger Deep vary.

No sunlight reaches below about 1,000 m. Creatures in these scenes are lit so you can see them. Mariana snailfish appear in the upper hadal zone, not at Challenger Deep.
